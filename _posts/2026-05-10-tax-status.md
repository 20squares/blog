If something's clear, that is that no one wants to pay tax. This is for several reasons, but in a nutshell, we may say that tax is perceived as theft: You are forced to give away your hard-earned money just to see it being inefficiently spent by an overly bureaucratic machine. No one would ever stomach that, and the result is that avoiding/reducing taxation is probably one of the biggest and most economically lucrative consulting business on planet Earth. Furthermore, tax avoidance causes huge problems to governments, which have to constantly fight to make sure people pay their taxes. This is terrible as it results in bureaucracy getting more intolerable every day, and as usual the majority of this burden falls on average Joe. Your billionaire acquaintance probably has an entire law firm working around the clock to shuffle his money around so that the net tax he pays year after year is zero, whereas you not only pay more than him, but are at an even greater risk of being considered at fault for reasons™, with very dire consequences.

## Veblen Goods and Status Signalling

One funny thing is how, among the very wealthy, many of the very same people that say "I don't wanna waste money on tax" then have absolutely no problem spending the equivalent of a downtown apartment on a timepiece or on a car. Now take it from someone that is very well into bespoke tailoring and shoemaking: Albeit attractive, it is undeniable how these goods are also -- per sé -- completely useless, as many luxury goods are. The function these goods provide is mainly **status signalling**: Your choice of timepiece says a lot about your economic status, ancenstry, aesthetic taste and class roots. You may be a nouveau riche or an old money guy, opt for quiet or loud signalling, but the luxury choices you make are in any case directed at validating and signalling that status to an audience of your choice.

Economically, I've always been attracted by the concept of [Veblen good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veblen_good). The **law of demand** is a fundamental concept in Economics. In a nutshell, it says that if something costs less, people want more of it. This is the whole mechanism behind discounts and sales: That fantastic TV you saw on Amazon is now 50% off, and it would be foolish not to buy it at this price... Veblen goods are a very small class of goods that spectacurarly violate this law: As price goes up, people want **more** of them, not less. Unsurprisingly, the majority of Veblen goods are luxury items. As they serve as tools for status signalling, their high price makes them more desirable. After all a Ferrari wouldn't be a Ferrari if any middle-class fuck could own one. The whole point of carrying around a whole downtown apartment on your wrist is probably to show to your peers the sheer level of fuck-you-money you own.

## Status Signalling via Taxation

Going back to taxes, people don't want to pay them because they see no real return on them. To this matter, let's postpone yapping about Tragedy of the Commons to another post. But what if taxation was to be transformed into a Veblen good? 

Imagine this. Every country has a hyerarchy of Awards and Decorations: Knighthoods, Nobel Prizes, Presidential Medals and the like. In general, the more accomplished you are in your line of work, the more it is likely that your government may wish to solidify your achievements with a medal. Now imagine a similar hyerarchy for tax contributions. You paid more than 1M/year for the last 10 years? You become a Knight of the Excheque. 10M/year? A Baronett, and so on and so forth.

Now imagine that, on top of this, each level of the hyearchy unlocks a series of benefits which cannot be acquired by any other mean. Suddenly, your trophy wife is begging you to pay MORE taxes otherwise she won't be able to take part to that very exclusive fashion show in Milan, or to the Met Gala. Rolex and Patek now produce limited editions which are available only to some of said contributors, and a series of elite clubs require this sort of status for access.

In such a system, taxation becomes the baseline indicator to access any other Veblen good. It is even more important than the timepiece you wear or than the car you drive, as it can fundamentally impact which car and timepiece you can buy in the first place. As such, and considering how pervasive the need for status signalling is, paying tax would probably become the most important social activity of the year. I can easily imagine a timeline where people are like "what? Did you REALLY move your legal residence to Montecarlo to avoid paying tax? That's so grossier, What kind of STRACCIONE must you be?" 

Implemented well, such policy results in a huge reduction of bureaucracy, as the highest potential contributors, which are also the hardest ones for the revenue office to chase, would start pouring money into the government's coffer out of their own volition. Big contributors get something in return for their tax money, small contributors get less bureaucracy, and everyone is happy.

## Some caveats

Such a policy, obviously, won't work everywhere. But in some places, such as Italy, the UK or the US, which have a very big tradition of luxury experiences and where the socialite culture is quite high, it would certainly work. I do not know if the UK or the US would ever consider something like this, tho. As for Italy, unfortunately, the problem is mostly cultural: Our whole left-wing tradition is very well set in the idea that wealth must be opposed at all costs: The wealthy individual is almost always considered a thief, ax exploiter, a capitalist pig, and for such reason, his achievements should not be really celebrated. I do not know how much of this is envy and how much is our Catholic culture of sin and guilt, but I'm fairly certain such policy would be fiercely opposed on moral grounds, with little interest for the fact that it could be the biggest economic enabler for the government's coffers since the Economic Miracle of the 50s. Pity!
